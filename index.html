Unit 1 · Class XI · Chapter 01

The Living World

What makes something "alive," how we name it, and how we sort the sprawling diversity of life into order.

§1

Diversity in the Living World

Our planet holds a staggering variety of living organisms — from microscopic bacteria to the blue whale, from mosses to giant sequoias. This range of variation is called biodiversity. So far, 1.7–1.8 million species have been identified and described, but the actual number of species estimated to exist on Earth is far higher — some estimates run to 8.7 million or more.

Nomenclature and identification become impossible without properly studying and describing this diversity. Every living organism needs a scientific name that is recognised the world over.
§2

What Is "Living"?

No single defining feature is sufficient on its own — living organisms are best recognised by a combination of the characteristics below.

01

Growth

Living organisms grow by an increase in cell number (division) or cell mass. In plants, growth by cell division continues throughout life at the meristems. Non-living objects (like a mountain or a crystal) can also "grow" by accumulation of material on the surface — so growth alone is not diagnostic of life.

02

Reproduction

Organisms reproduce, i.e., give rise to progeny that resemble the parent. Interestingly, reproduction is not universal — mules, sterile worker bees, and infertile human couples are alive but do not reproduce. So reproduction, too, is not a defining feature by itself.

03

Metabolism

All living organisms are made of chemicals which continuously undergo transformation within the body — the sum total of these reactions is metabolism, and it includes countless biochemical reactions occurring simultaneously in every living cell. No non-living object shows metabolism, which makes it the closest thing to a defining, unique feature. Metabolism can even be demonstrated in vitro, in cell-free systems — meaning isolated metabolic reactions occurring outside the body are not living things, even though they are metabolic reactions. This is why cellular organisation is added as a further criterion.

04

Cellular Organisation

All living organisms show a defined and precise cellular organisation — a fundamental feature of every living organism, unicellular or multicellular.

05

Consciousness / Response to Stimuli

The ability to sense the environment and respond to physical, chemical, or biological stimuli is shown by all living organisms — from single-celled organisms to the most complex ones. In multicellular organisms this is achieved through a nervous system. Consciousness is thus considered the defining property of living organisms.

Overall: metabolism and consciousness (response to stimuli), taken together, are considered the defining features of life.
§3

Taxonomy & Systematics

Classifying organisms requires characterisation, identification, classification, and nomenclature — together, these processes form the subject matter of taxonomy.

Systematics comes from the Latin "systema," meaning the systematic arrangement of organisms — Linnaeus used Systema Naturae as the title of his own publication. Systematics accounts not only for diversity but also for the evolutionary relationships between organisms.

§4

Nomenclature — Naming the Living

With millions of plants and animals in the world, every organism needs a scientific name so people everywhere, speaking different languages, know exactly which organism is meant.

Binomial Nomenclature

Biologists follow a common naming system called binomial nomenclature, introduced by Carolus Linnaeus. A scientific name has two parts — the generic name and the specific epithet. E.g. Mangifera indica (mango): Mangifera is the genus, indica the specific epithet.

Universal Rules of Nomenclature

  • Biological names are generally in Latin and written in italics — being Latinised or derived from Latin, they're treated as Latin words regardless of origin.
  • The first word denotes the genus; the second denotes the specific epithet.
  • Both words, when handwritten, are separately underlined (or printed in italics) to show their Latin origin.
  • The genus name starts with a capital letter; the specific epithet starts with a small letter — e.g. Mangifera indica.
  • The author's name follows the specific epithet in abbreviated form, e.g. Mangifera indica Linn. — showing it was first described by Linnaeus.
§5

Taxonomic Categories & Hierarchy

Classification is not a single step but a set of hierarchical steps, each representing a rank or category — hence a taxonomic category. All categories together form the taxonomic hierarchy.

Each unit in the hierarchy is called a taxon (plural: taxa), with a fixed position from broadest to narrowest:

Kingdom
the broadest assemblage — organisms sharing very few common features
Phylum / Division
animals grouped into phyla; plants into divisions
Class
a phylum/division comprising related orders
Order
a group of related, though different, families
Family
based on aggregates of characters — vegetative & reproductive in plants
Genus
a group of related species with more similarities than species of another genus
Species
the lowest category — individuals with fundamental similarities, actually or potentially interbreeding
Species is the basic taxonomic category. It refers to a group of individuals with fundamental similarities; members show close resemblance compared to members of other species. Minor variation exists within a species too — humans show a lot of variation, yet all belong to one species, Homo sapiens.

§6

Taxonomical Aids

Knowledge gained from taxonomic studies is useful in agriculture, forestry, industry, and general biology — the methods, techniques, and procedures built around this are called taxonomical aids: ways of preserving material and keeping records.

🌿 Herbarium

A storehouse of collected plant specimens, dried, pressed, and preserved on sheets, arranged by a widely accepted classification system. Each sheet carries a label with date/place of collection, name, family, and collector.

🏞️ Botanical Gardens

Gardens where living plants are grown for reference, each labelled with scientific name and family. Famous examples: Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew (England); Indian Botanical Garden, Howrah.

🏛️ Museum

Plant and animal specimens are collected, preserved (in preservative solution, or dried), and kept for study and reference — animal specimens are sometimes preserved as mounted skeletons too.

🦁 Zoological Parks

Places where live animals are kept in a protected environment, allowing us to study their food habits and behaviour.

🔑 Key

A tool for identifying plants and animals based on similarities and dissimilarities — usually analytical, made of contrasting statements called couplets. Each statement is a lead. Keys are usually dichotomous, and a separate key is needed for each taxonomic category.

📖 Manuals, Monographs, Catalogues & Floras

Flora: the actual account of habitat and distribution of plants in a given area. Manuals: help identify species found in an area. Monographs: contain information on any one taxon. Catalogues: give correct scientific names and citations, with authors' names.

§7

Quick Recap

  1. Growth, reproduction, metabolism, cellular organisation, and consciousness together characterise "living" — no single feature is sufficient alone.
  2. Taxonomy = classifying, naming, and identifying organisms; systematics also considers evolutionary relationships.
  3. Binomial nomenclature (Linnaeus): genus + specific epithet, italicised, genus capitalised, epithet lower-case, followed by author abbreviation.
  4. Taxonomic hierarchy, broadest to narrowest: Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
  5. Species is the fundamental/basic unit of classification.
  6. Taxonomical aids: herbarium, botanical gardens, museums, zoological parks, keys, manuals, monographs, catalogues, and floras.

Unit 1 · Class XI · Chapter 02

Biological Classification

From two kingdoms to five (and beyond) — how Whittaker's system sorts Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, plus the strange, borderline case of viruses.

§1

Why the Two-Kingdom System Failed

Linnaeus's original two kingdom classification placed all organisms into Plantae or Animalia, based mainly on whether they were mobile, how they got nutrition, and whether they had a cell wall. It worked reasonably well for large, familiar organisms — but it broke down for the microscopic world.

Problem: the two-kingdom system did not distinguish between eukaryotes and prokaryotes, unicellular and multicellular organisms, or photosynthetic (autotrophic) and non-photosynthetic (heterotrophic) organisms. Fungi, for instance, don't fit comfortably into either "plant" or "animal."

This is why newer systems of classification were proposed — most influentially, R. H. Whittaker's Five Kingdom Classification (1969).

§2

Whittaker's Five Kingdom System

Whittaker's classification uses five criteria to place organisms: cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction, and phylogenetic relationships. On this basis, all organisms are placed in one of five kingdoms:

Monera
all prokaryotes — bacteria, cyanobacteria, mycoplasma
Protista
unicellular eukaryotes — chrysophytes, dinoflagellates, euglenoids, slime moulds, protozoans
Fungi
heterotrophic, chiefly saprophytic eukaryotes with a chitin cell wall
Plantae
multicellular, autotrophic eukaryotes with a cellulose cell wall
Animalia
multicellular, heterotrophic eukaryotes without a cell wall
NEET tip: Kingdoms Monera and Protista may be autotrophic or heterotrophic — nutrition mode alone does not define them; it's cell structure (prokaryotic vs eukaryotic) plus body organisation that separates them first.
§3

Kingdom Monera

Bacteria are the sole members of Kingdom Monera and the most abundant micro-organisms. They occur almost everywhere — soil, water, air, hot springs, deep-sea vents, and inside other organisms — and vary widely in shape: coccus (spherical), bacillus (rod-shaped), vibrium (comma-shaped), and spirillum (spiral).

Archaebacteria vs Eubacteria

Archaebacteria
Cell wall Distinct chemistry from other bacteria — allows survival in extreme conditions
Habitat Extreme salty areas (halophiles), hot springs (thermoacidophiles), marshy areas (methanogens)
Note Methanogens live in the gut of ruminant animals (e.g. cattle) and produce methane from dung
Eubacteria ("true bacteria")
Cell wall Rigid, and if motile, a flagellum is present
Types Cyanobacteria, chemosynthetic autotrophic bacteria, heterotrophic bacteria
Note Far more common than archaebacteria

Three Functional Groups of Eubacteria

01

Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae)

Photosynthetic autotrophs with chlorophyll a (like plants); unicellular, colonial, or filamentous, often found as blooms in fresh water. Many fix atmospheric nitrogen in specialised cells called heterocysts (e.g. Nostoc, Anabaena).

02

Chemosynthetic autotrophic bacteria

Oxidise inorganic substances such as nitrates, nitrites, and ammonia to obtain energy for their ATP production, and use this energy to make their own food. They play a vital role in recycling nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, and iron.

03

Heterotrophic bacteria

The most abundant of all — mostly decomposers, important as fermenters, nitrogen fixers, or pathogens causing diseases like cholera, typhoid, tetanus, and citrus canker.

Bacterial reproduction: mainly by fission; under unfavourable conditions, bacteria produce spores to withstand extremes. Genetic recombination is also shown, via a primitive sex-like process.

Mycoplasma

Organisms that completely lack a cell wall and are the smallest living cells known — they can survive without oxygen. Many are pathogenic in animals and plants.

§4

Kingdom Protista

All single-celled eukaryotes are placed here. Protists form a link with other eukaryotic kingdoms (Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) — most have flagella or cilia at some stage, and reproduce both asexually and sexually via cell fusion and zygote formation.

🟡 Chrysophytes

Includes diatoms and golden algae (desmids); mostly found in fresh water and marine environments. Cell walls form two thin overlapping shells that fit together like a soap box — indestructible, leaving behind "diatomaceous earth" deposits.

🔴 Dinoflagellates

Mostly marine and photosynthetic, with two flagella (one longitudinal, one transverse) lying in furrows on the body. Cell wall has stiff cellulose plates. Rapid multiplication causes "red tides" (e.g. Gonyaulax) that can turn the sea red/orange and release toxins harmful to fish.

🟢 Euglenoids

Mostly fresh-water organisms found in stagnant water; no cell wall, instead a protein-rich layer called a pellicle, making them flexible. Have two flagella (one short, one long). Autotrophic in light, but heterotrophic when deprived of sunlight — e.g. Euglena.

🟠 Slime Moulds

Saprophytic protists that produce spores for reproduction. The body moves along decaying twigs/leaves engulfing organic material as an aggregation called a plasmodium, which grows and fragments under favourable conditions to form fruiting bodies bearing spores with true walls.

Protozoans — all heterotrophic, living as predators or parasites

Example Amoeba, Entamoeba (marine forms have silica shells)

§5

Kingdom Fungi

Fungi are heterotrophic, chiefly saprophytic organisms that grow as mycelia made of thread-like hyphae. Their cell walls are made of chitin (not cellulose), and most are multicellular except yeast, which is unicellular. Some are parasitic and live off living plants and animals; some are symbionts, living with algae as lichens or with plant roots as mycorrhiza.

Habitat: fungi are cosmopolitan and live in air, water, soil, and on animals and plants; they prefer warm and humid places (this is why bread, leather, and pickles are more easily spoiled in the rainy season).

Reproduction

Occurs by vegetative means (fragmentation, fission, budding), asexual means (spores called conidia or sporangiospores), and sexual means (oospores, ascospores, basidiospores). Sexual reproduction has three distinct stages: plasmogamy (fusion of protoplasms), karyogamy (fusion of two nuclei), and meiosis in the zygote to restore the haploid number.

Classes of Fungi

Phycomycetes
Habitat Aquatic, decaying wood, moist places, obligate parasites on plants
Mycelium Aseptate and coenocytic
Example Mucor, Rhizopus (bread mould), Albugo
Ascomycetes ("sac fungi")
Habitat Mostly multicellular, some unicellular (yeast); decomposers, saprophytes, parasitic, coprophilous
Spores Sexual spores called ascospores produced endogenously in sac-like asci
Example Saccharomyces (yeast), Neurospora, Penicillium
Basidiomycetes
Habitat Soil, logs, tree stumps, living plant bodies (rusts and smuts)
Spores Sex organs absent; produce basidiospores exogenously on a basidium
Example Mushrooms (Agaricus), bracket fungi, puffballs
Deuteromycetes ("imperfect fungi")
Habitat Reproduce only by asexual spores called conidia; sexual stage unknown/absent
Note Once a sexual stage is discovered, they're moved to the appropriate class
Example Alternaria, Colletotrichum, Trichoderma

Lichens & Mycorrhiza — symbiotic associations

Lichens are a symbiotic association between algae (the phycobiont, which prepares food) and fungi (the mycobiont, which provides shelter and absorbs water/minerals). They are excellent pollution indicators — they do not grow in polluted areas. Mycorrhiza is a symbiotic association between fungi and the roots of higher plants — the fungi help absorb water and minerals, while the plant provides sugars and nitrogenous compounds.

§6

Kingdoms Plantae & Animalia — a Quick Preview

Both kingdoms are eukaryotic and multicellular, but differ sharply in nutrition and structure — these two kingdoms are explored in full in the next two NCERT chapters (Plant Kingdom and Animal Kingdom).

Plantae
Nutrition Autotrophic (photosynthetic), a few partially heterotrophic (insectivorous, parasitic)
Cell wall Present — made of cellulose
Reserve food Starch
Animalia
Nutrition Heterotrophic — holozoic (ingestion)
Cell wall Absent

§7

Viruses, Viroids & Prions — Outside All Five Kingdoms

Viruses are so different from typical cellular organisms that Whittaker's system does not include them at all — they are acellular and obligate parasites, non-living outside a host cell yet capable of "hijacking" host machinery to replicate inside it.

D. J. Ivanowsky (1892) first reported a contagion smaller than bacteria that caused the tobacco mosaic disease, passing through a bacteria-proof filter. M. W. Beijerinck (1898) showed the fluid was infectious only when it could multiply within living cells, and named it contagium vivum fluidum (contagious living fluid). W. M. Stanley (1935) showed that viruses could be crystallised, and that crystals consist largely of protein.

Structure: a virus has a protein coat, called a capsid, made of small subunits called capsomeres. The capsid encloses genetic material — either DNA or RNA, never both — which may be single- or double-stranded.
🦠 Viroids (T. O. Diener, 1971)

Smaller than viruses; consist of free RNA only, with no protein coat at all. The RNA has low molecular weight. Discovered while working on potato spindle tuber disease.

🧬 Prions

Abnormally folded proteins (no nucleic acid at all) that cause serious neurological diseases in mammals, such as bovine spongiform encephalopathy ("mad cow disease") and its human variant, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD).

§8

Quick Recap

  1. Two-kingdom system failed because it didn't separate prokaryote/eukaryote, unicellular/ multicellular, or autotroph/heterotroph.
  2. Whittaker's Five Kingdom system (1969): Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia — based on cell structure, body organisation, nutrition, reproduction, phylogeny.
  3. Monera = all prokaryotes; split into archaebacteria (extremophiles) and eubacteria (cyanobacteria, chemosynthetic, heterotrophic).
  4. Protista = unicellular eukaryotes: chrysophytes, dinoflagellates, euglenoids, slime moulds, protozoans (amoeboid, flagellated, ciliated, sporozoan).
  5. Fungi = heterotrophic, chitin cell wall; classes Phycomycetes, Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes, Deuteromycetes; lichens (fungus + alga) and mycorrhiza (fungus + root) are symbioses.
  6. Viruses are acellular, contain either DNA or RNA (never both) enclosed in a capsid; viroids have RNA with no protein coat; prions are infectious proteins with no nucleic acid.

Unit 3 · Class XI · Chapter 08

Cell: The Unit of Life

Every organism is built from cells — from the cell theory to the machinery inside a single eukaryotic cell: membranes, organelles, and the nucleus that runs the show.

§1

The Cell Theory

Robert Hooke first observed and named "cells" in 1665, while examining a thin slice of cork under a self-designed microscope — the boxes he saw were actually dead cell walls. Building on decades of microscopy by scientists like Purkinje and von Mohl, Schleiden (1838), a botanist, and Schwann (1839), a zoologist, together proposed the cell theory, later expanded by Rudolf Virchow (1855).

Cell Theory (final form):
1. All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula e cellula — Virchow).

Cells vary enormously in shape and size — from a mycoplasma just 0.3 μm long, to a single ostrich egg (the largest isolated single cell), to the longest cell being a nerve cell. Cell size and shape are usually linked to specific functions.

§2

Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells

All cells share three common features: a plasma membrane as the outer-most boundary, a nucleus or nucleoid containing genetic material, and cytoplasm. Based on nuclear organisation, all organisms are grouped into two broad categories: prokaryotes and eukaryotes.

Prokaryotic cell
Nucleus Absent — genetic material lies in an unbound region: the nucleoid
Size Generally smaller (0.1–5.0 μm) than eukaryotic cells
Organelles Membrane-bound organelles absent; only ribosomes present (70S)
Examples Bacteria, cyanobacteria, mycoplasma, PPLO
Eukaryotic cell
Nucleus Present — a well-defined nucleus bound by a nuclear envelope
Size Generally larger (5–100 μm)
Organelles Membrane-bound organelles present (ER, Golgi, mitochondria, etc.); ribosomes 80S
Examples Protists, fungi, plants, animals

Structure of a Prokaryotic Cell

Besides the nucleoid, prokaryotic cells may possess a loose circular DNA called a plasmid, which confers special phenotypic traits like antibiotic resistance — used heavily in genetic engineering. Additional surface structures include:

01

Glycocalyx

May be a loose slime layer or a thick, tough capsule outside the cell wall.

02

Cell wall & plasma membrane

The cell wall gives shape; beneath it, the plasma membrane is closely surrounded by it, together called the cell envelope.

03

Flagella, pili & fimbriae

Flagella are locomotory; pili and fimbriae don't aid movement but help attachment to surfaces or to other cells for DNA transfer (conjugation).

04

Ribosomes & inclusion bodies

70S ribosomes, made of 50S + 30S subunits, occur in large numbers attached to the plasma membrane and are the sites of protein synthesis. Reserve material is stored in inclusion bodies (e.g. phosphate granules, gas vacuoles).

§3

Cell Envelope & the Plasma Membrane

In eukaryotic cells too, the cell envelope consists of the cell membrane, and (in plants, fungi, bacteria) a cell wall lying just outside it. The plasma membrane is universal to all cells and is selectively permeable, controlling what moves in and out.

Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicolson, 1972): the widely accepted model for membrane structure. Membrane lipids (phospholipids) are arranged in a bilayer, quasi-fluid, allowing lateral movement of proteins within it — hence "fluid." The membrane proteins are of two main types, integral (embedded partially or wholly) and peripheral (lying on the surface).

Cell Wall

Present in plant cells and fungi (but not animal cells), lying outside the plasma membrane. Made mainly of cellulose in plants; also contains other polysaccharides, proteins. Provides structural strength, protects the protoplasm, and (along with the middle lamella made of calcium pectate) helps cells stick together. Cell walls also facilitate a special mode of transport called plasmodesmata, which connects the cytoplasm of adjacent cells.

§4

The Endomembrane System

Organelles whose functions are coordinated (though structurally different) form the endomembrane system: the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and vacuoles. Mitochondria, chloroplasts, and peroxisomes are not part of this system since their functions aren't coordinated with the others.

🕸️ Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

A network of membranous tubules and cisternae, dividing the cytoplasm into compartments. Rough ER (RER) bears ribosomes on its surface and is active in protein synthesis and secretion. Smooth ER (SER) lacks ribosomes and is the principal site for lipid/steroid synthesis.

📦 Golgi Apparatus (Camillo Golgi, 1898)

Made of flattened, membrane-bound sacs called cisternae stacked parallel to each other, with a cis face (forming face, near ER) and a trans face (maturing face). Its major function is packaging materials, often synthesised by the ER, into vesicles for secretion — hence "packaging and dispatch."

💧 Lysosomes

Membrane-bound vesicular structures formed by the Golgi apparatus, containing powerful hydrolytic enzymes (lipases, proteases, carbohydrases) active in acidic conditions, capable of breaking down almost all organic material — hence called "suicide bags" of the cell, since they can also digest the cell itself under abnormal conditions.

🫧 Vacuoles

Membrane-bound (by a single membrane called the tonoplast) spaces in the cytoplasm containing water, sap, excretory products, and other materials. In plants, the vacuole may occupy up to 90% of cell volume, providing turgidity and rigidity.

§5

Mitochondria & Plastids — the "Power Houses"

Mitochondria (discovered by Kolliker) are membrane-bound structures found in almost all eukaryotic cells, with a double membrane — the outer smooth, the inner folded into cristae which increase surface area for ATP generation. Since ATP is the energy currency of the cell, mitochondria are called the "power houses" of the cell. They are semi-autonomous: they contain their own circular DNA, RNA, and ribosomes (70S), allowing them to divide by fission and synthesise some of their own proteins.

Plastids are found in all plant cells and in euglenoids, and also have a double-membrane envelope with their own DNA and ribosomes.

Chloroplasts
Pigments Chlorophyll a & b, carotenoids
Function Site of photosynthesis
Structure Grana (stacks of thylakoids) embedded in the stroma
Chromoplasts
Pigments Fat-soluble carotenoid pigments (carotene, xanthophylls)
Function Impart yellow/orange/red colour to flowers and fruits
Leucoplasts
Pigments Colourless
Function Store nutrients — amyloplasts (starch), elaioplasts (oils/fats), aleuroplasts (proteins)
§6

Ribosomes, the Cytoskeleton & Cell Motility

Ribosomes were discovered by George Palade and are the site of protein synthesis — found either freely in the cytoplasm or attached to the ER/nuclear membrane. Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S (50S + 30S); eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S (60S + 40S), though eukaryotic organelle ribosomes (mitochondria, chloroplasts) remain 70S — a very common NEET distractor.

The cytoskeleton is a network of protein filaments — microtubules, microfilaments, and intermediate filaments — that maintains cell shape, motility, and organelle movement, and helps in cell division.

01

Cilia & Flagella

Both are hair-like or whip-like outgrowths of the cell surface, with the same basic organisation — a bundle of microtubules arranged as 9+2 (nine peripheral doublets + two central singlets), covered by the plasma membrane. Cilia beat rapidly to move fluid/particles past a fixed cell; flagella have a similar structure but generate propulsive movement of the entire cell (e.g. sperm).

02

Centrosome & Centrioles

Found in animal cells (and some fungi/algae) — usually contains two cylindrical structures called centrioles, surrounded by amorphous pericentriolar material. Each centriole has a cartwheel-like array of nine peripheral microtubule triplets — the centrioles form the basal body of cilia/flagella, and organise the spindle fibres during cell division.

§7

Nucleus & Chromosomes

Discovered by Robert Brown (1831), the nucleus is generally spherical, bound by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope, perforated by nuclear pores that allow the exchange of RNA and protein between the nucleus and cytoplasm. It contains a fibrous network called nucleoplasm/chromatin, and a dense spherical body — the nucleolus — which is the site of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis.

Chromatin vs Chromosomes: chromatin is the loosely-packed, thread-like form of nuclear material seen during interphase; during cell division, it condenses into compact, visible chromosomes. Both are made of the same components — DNA and histone proteins.

Structure of a Chromosome

Each chromosome has a primary constriction called the centromere, on either side of which lie disc-shaped structures called kinetochores — the sites of attachment of spindle fibres during cell division. Based on the position of the centromere, chromosomes are classified into four types:

Metacentric
centromere in the middle, giving two equal arms
Sub-metacentric
centromere slightly away from the middle, giving one shorter and one longer arm
Acrocentric
centromere very close to one end, giving one very short and one very long arm
Telocentric
centromere at the terminal end of the chromosome
§8

Quick Recap

  1. Cell theory (Schleiden, Schwann, Virchow): all organisms are made of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells arise from pre-existing cells.
  2. Prokaryotes lack a true nucleus (have a nucleoid) and membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotes have both.
  3. The plasma membrane follows the fluid mosaic model (Singer & Nicolson) — a fluid phospholipid bilayer with integral and peripheral proteins.
  4. Endomembrane system = ER + Golgi apparatus + lysosomes + vacuoles (coordinated function); mitochondria and plastids are semi-autonomous, with their own DNA and 70S ribosomes.
  5. Cilia/flagella have a 9+2 microtubule arrangement; centrioles have a 9+0 (triplet) arrangement and form the basal body.
  6. The nucleus (Robert Brown) contains chromatin, which condenses into chromosomes during division; centromere position gives four chromosome types — metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, telocentric.
